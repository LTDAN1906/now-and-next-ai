Stay safe

Simple habits that stop most AI scams and mistakes.

Practical steps based on the failures above and on advice from the FBI, the FTC and security researchers.

Scams and deepfakes

  • Agree on a family code word

    Scammers can use AI to copy a loved one’s voice. Pick a secret word or phrase with your family, and ask for it if a “relative” calls in a panic asking for money.

    Real examples: A fake Biden robocall tells New Hampshire voters to stay home

    Source: FBI

  • Hang up and call back

    Don’t trust a voice or a face on a screen by itself, even if it seems exactly right. If someone pressures you for money or information, hang up and call back on a number you already know is real.

    Real examples: A deepfake video call tricks a finance worker into sending $25 million

    Sources: FTC, FBI

  • Be wary of payments you can’t undo

    Be very suspicious if someone asks you to pay by wire transfer, cryptocurrency or gift cards. Scammers prefer them because the money is hard to get back.

    Source: FTC

  • Look and listen closely

    AI-made images and video can have tells like distorted hands or odd teeth and eyes, and a cloned voice can sound slightly off in tone or word choice. These clues are getting harder to spot, so use them alongside the other checks, not instead of them.

    Real examples: A deepfake video call tricks a finance worker into sending $25 million

    Source: FBI

  • Share less of your face and voice

    The less video and audio of you that’s public, the less scammers have to copy. Consider making your social media accounts private and limiting followers to people you know.

    Source: FBI

Using AI tools

  • Keep secrets out of chatbots

    Don’t paste passwords, financial details, medical records or confidential work files into an AI chatbot. Check its privacy settings too: ChatGPT and Claude both let you choose whether your chats are used to train their models.

    Real examples: A ChatGPT bug shows users other people’s chat titles; Moltbook, a social network for AI agents, leaks 1.5 million API keys

    Sources: OpenAI, Anthropic

  • Double-check answers that matter

    AI can state made-up facts with total confidence. Before you rely on an answer for money, health, legal or safety decisions, check it against a trusted source.

    Real examples: Lawyers are fined for citing court cases ChatGPT made up; Air Canada has to honor a refund policy its chatbot made up; Deloitte refunds Australia over a report with AI-made errors

  • Give AI agents as little access as possible

    If you let an AI agent use your computer, email or accounts, give it only the access the task needs, keep backups, and make it ask before it deletes, sends or buys anything.

    Real examples: An AI coding agent wipes a startup’s database in 9 seconds; Replit’s AI agent deletes a production database during a code freeze

    Source: OWASP

  • Watch what your AI assistant reads

    Hidden instructions in an email, document or web page can trick an AI assistant into leaking data or doing things it shouldn’t. This is called prompt injection. Think twice before letting an assistant that can see your private data read messages or pages from strangers.

    Real examples: EchoLeak: a zero-click flaw in Microsoft 365 Copilot

    Source: OWASP

If something goes wrong

  • Report it

    In the U.S., report AI-powered scams and fraud to the FBI’s Internet Crime Complaint Center and to the FTC. Reports help investigators spot patterns and warn others.

    Sources: FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center, FTC ReportFraud

AI explained

Plain-English answers, no background needed.

What is a large language model?

A large language model, or LLM, is an AI system trained on an enormous amount of text: books, websites, code and more. During training it learns to predict the next word in a passage, over and over, billions of times.

That simple goal turns out to be powerful. To predict text well, a model has to pick up grammar, facts and patterns of reasoning. The result can write, summarize, translate, answer questions and write code.

ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini are all built on large language models. Newer ones also handle images, audio and video, which is why you’ll often hear them called multimodal.

What’s the difference between a chatbot and an AI agent?

A chatbot answers you one message at a time. You ask, it replies, and you decide what happens next.

An agent is given a goal and works toward it on its own. It might search the web, open files, use apps, or write and run code, checking its progress along the way. Models that can operate a computer directly, like GPT-6 Astra, are built for this kind of work.

Agents can save a lot of time, but they also raise new questions: how much should an AI do without asking, and who is responsible when it makes a mistake?

What does “open-weight” mean?

Every AI model is defined by its weights, the billions of numbers it learned during training. An open-weight model publishes those numbers, so anyone can download the model, run it on their own hardware and change it. Meta’s Muse Glimmer is one example.

Closed models, like OpenAI’s and Anthropic’s flagship models, keep their weights private. You use them through an app or a paid connection to the company’s servers.

Open models give people more control and privacy. Closed models are often more capable, and their makers can more easily add and enforce safety rules.

Why do AI models make things up?

Language models generate text by predicting what’s likely to come next. They don’t look facts up unless they’re connected to a search tool, so when a model doesn’t know something, it can still produce a confident-sounding answer that’s wrong. This is called a hallucination.

Newer models hallucinate less, and many can now search the web and cite sources. It still pays to check anything important, which is why every story on this site links to where the information came from.

Glossary

Agent
An AI system that works toward a goal on its own, taking a series of steps such as searching, using apps or writing code, instead of just answering one message.
AGI (artificial general intelligence)
AI that could match people across most kinds of thinking work. There’s no agreed definition, and experts disagree about when, or whether, it will arrive.
Benchmark
A standard test used to compare AI models, such as a set of coding problems or exam questions.
Chain of thought
The step-by-step reasoning a model works through before it answers. Researchers study it to understand, and check, how a model reached a result.
Computer use
An AI model’s ability to operate a computer like a person: reading the screen, clicking, typing and moving between apps.
Context window
How much text, images or files a model can consider at once, measured in tokens. A bigger window lets it work with longer documents.
Fine-tuning
Extra training that adapts an existing model to a specific task, style or subject.
GPU
Graphics processing unit: a chip first built for video games that turned out to be ideal for training and running AI.
Hallucination
When an AI model states something false or made-up as if it were true.
Inference
Running a trained model to get an answer. Every chatbot reply is inference.
Large language model (LLM)
An AI model trained on huge amounts of text to predict the next word, which lets it write, summarize, translate and answer questions.
Multimodal
Able to work with more than one kind of input or output, such as text, images, audio and video.
Neural network
A kind of computer program loosely inspired by the brain, made of layers of simple connected units that learn patterns from data.
Open-weight model
A model whose trained weights are published, so anyone can download, run and modify it.
Parameters
The internal numbers a model learns during training. Model size is often described by its parameter count, like “30 billion parameters.”
Prompt
The instructions or question you give an AI model.
Reasoning model
A model that spends extra time working through a problem step by step before answering, which helps with math, science and coding.
Token
A small chunk of text, often a word or part of a word. Models read and write in tokens, and usage and pricing are usually counted in them.
Training
Teaching a model by showing it huge amounts of data and adjusting its parameters until its outputs improve.
Transformer
The neural network design, introduced in 2017, that almost all of today’s major language models are built on.
